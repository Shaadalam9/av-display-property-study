# Personalise display amount, fix display meaning

The literature on in-vehicle explanation displays for automated vehicles (AVs) has varied about 35 distinct visual properties, but only a handful in isolation, and almost all of the controlled evidence comes from one research line (Colley, Jansen, Rukzio and colleagues at Ulm) using online video studies with passenger viewpoints. Three findings organise everything else. First, **properties that carry meaning have reached consensus and should be fixed**: AR windshield placement beats tablets or centre screens, explanations must come before the action, a single path line beats double lines or cones, hue (not transparency) should code uncertainty, and highlights must not flicker. Second, **properties that set how much is shown and how loudly show real between-person disagreement**: highlight opacity, highlight scope, ego-path visibility, information density and display persistence. Here, experts disagreed (Jansen et al. 2025 expert SDs up to 0.26 on a 0–1 scale), optimised designs stayed spread across people over three days, and the preferred amount of information flipped with users' initial trust ([Jansen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1); [Colley et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28552); [Hartwich et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2021.669030/full)). Third, **adding layers carries a measurable cost**: other road users' predicted trajectories raised mental workload (12.40 vs 9.89) and perceived clutter (4.16 vs 2.53 on a 7-point scale), and showing the ego path lowered trust in one study but raised it in another ([Colley et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3534609); [Colley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3631408)). Prior human-in-the-loop optimisers either used rich queries and few dimensions, or many dimensions with sparse coverage: Jansen et al. (2025) tuned 16 parameters in 10–15 rated iterations, while pairwise-query studies stay at roughly 3–7 dimensions. That supports the planned design: screen 15 video-editable properties with Best-Worst Scaling (BWS) and A/B clips, then personalise about 4 continuous or ordinal ones. No prior AV display study screened properties formally before optimising, so the screening stage is itself a contribution.

## Thirty-five properties, one dominant lab, mostly passive video

The evidence base is narrower than its volume suggests. The best-controlled studies are a 2×2×2 detection × prediction × planning study on a simulated AR windshield (N=216) ([Colley et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3534609)), a 13-condition path shape × colour × transparency study (N=280) plus a 2×2 real-video study (N=238) ([Colley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3631408)), a semantic-segmentation highlighting study (N=32 + 41) ([Colley et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445351)), and a Bayesian-optimisation study (N=117) ([Jansen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1)). All are online and video-based with a passenger view, which is close to the planned crowdsourced format and makes their effects directly transferable. Outside that line, the key controlled contributions are explanation content and timing (Koo et al. 2015, N=64; Du et al. 2019, N=32) ([Koo et al. 2015](https://www.wendyju.com/publications/WhyDidMyCarJustDoThat.pdf); [Du et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.08878)), permanent vs adaptive HMIs split by trust (N=50) ([Hartwich et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-dynamics/articles/10.3389/fhumd.2021.669030/full)), and an on-road comparison of constant vs risk-triggered explanations ([Kim et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3610886)).

Outcomes split along a consistent seam. **Highlighting detected road users reliably raises subjective understanding** (SART 21.02 vs 16.61 with segmentation highlighting) **without changing trust** (3.32 vs 3.31) or mental load ([Colley et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445351)). Trust moves mainly when the whole design is tuned (the optimised designs of Jansen et al. 2025 raised trust from 3.04 for the expert design to 3.94) or with repeated exposure ([Jansen et al. 2025, TRF](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2025.05.013)). Workload and clutter come from adding layers, especially predicted trajectories of others, from many hues, from flicker, and from highlighting static infrastructure. They do not come from the ego path or from highlighting pedestrians and vehicles ([Colley et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3534609); [Jansen et al. 2025, TRF](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trf.2025.05.013)). A recurring caution matters for choosing objectives: several authors report **overtrust**. A visualisation judged negatively dragged down judgements of the AV's ability ([Colley et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3534609)), and wrong explanations lowered ratings of identical driving ([Kaufman et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05731v3)). Optimising purely for trust or perceived safety can therefore reward reassurance over understanding. The study should keep an understanding or predictability measure alongside preference.

The tables below are the de-duplicated catalogue. "Evidence" uses four grades. **Strong**: two or more controlled studies with a consistent direction, at least one read in full text. **Moderate**: one controlled study read in full, or several with conflicting direction. **Weak**: abstract-only or secondary reports, design rationales, or open-ended comments. **Gap**: no manipulation found. "Personalise vs fix" records whether the evidence shows between-person disagreement (personalise) or a clear best level (fix). Low/high levels marked "suggested" are this report's proposals, not tested values. "Editable in clip" judges whether the property can be varied convincingly as an overlay rendered in post-production on a pre-recorded first-person driving video, and whether its intended effect can be perceived by a passive viewer.

Two de-duplications matter. First, "information density" (E2) overlaps with per-element visibility (P1, R8, V3, V4). In the screening stage it should be run as a bundled ordinal ladder, and the per-element toggles kept only where they also carry a continuous salience parameter. Second, ego-path and highlight "visibility" become, as in Jansen et al. (2025), the lower end of an opacity dimension: opacity below a threshold means off ([Jansen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1)). This also suits pairwise optimisation, which works best on continuous axes.

## Sixteen-parameter optimisers worked only with sparse coverage

**No prior study screened display properties formally before optimising.** Jansen et al. (2025) took their elements from prior Ulm work, using Colley's split into detection, prediction and planning, and its parameters were the visibility, alpha and size of 7 elements. That gave 16 dimensions: 7 Boolean toggles (modelled as continuous and thresholded at 0.5) and 9 continuous values. Bounds were set so elements never became invisible (alpha ≥0.1) or overlapped. Colour, position and some alphas were excluded on semantic grounds. Eight experts supplied the parameter *values* for the baseline design but not the parameter *selection* ([Jansen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1)). The optimiser was rating-based multi-objective Bayesian optimisation (qEHVI in BoTorch, settings taken from Chan et al.). It ran 5 sampling plus 10 optimisation iterations on 33-second rides, and maximised safety, trust, predictability, acceptance and aesthetics while minimising mental demand. It beat both expert and user-designed displays on perceived safety, trust, predictability and cognitive load. Yet 21% of participants hit the perfect-score early stop, many elements ended switched off, and the authors could not even plot the Pareto front at 16 dimensions ([Jansen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1)). With 10–15 evaluations in 16 dimensions, the optimiser mostly explored which elements were on or off. The three-day follow-up kept the same 16 parameters and found that **between-person dispersion "remained substantial across days"**, while warm starts reached about 95% of their gain in about 7 evaluations without a better final design ([Colley et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.28552)).

The broader HCI record is more conservative about dimensionality when each query is weak. Chan et al. optimised **4 continuous parameters** with 40 evaluations per designer, the first 10 random. They catalogue three ways of shrinking spaces: eliminate parameters using the literature or small pilots, divide and conquer, or reduce with a model ([Chan et al. 2022](https://www.pokristensson.com/pubs/ChanEtAlCHI2022.pdf)). Koyama et al. argue that **pairwise comparisons carry little information per query**. They replaced them with slider queries for 3- to 7-dimensional crowd problems run over 15 iterations of 7 micro-tasks, and they report that Brochu's pairwise method handled 4- and 12-dimensional animation spaces only with domain priors ([Koyama et al. 2017](https://koyama.xyz/project/sequential_line_search/download/preprint.pdf)). Plane-search galleries reached 10–12 dimensions in 4–10 iterations, but the authors left pairwise BO out of the comparison because it "consistently requires much more iterations" ([Koyama et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.04107)). Population priors cut per-user effort to about 6–8 iterations ([Li et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15355)), and group-level data can initialise new users ([Liao et al. 2024](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/5cceb0f1-3be8-4d3b-a798-ec505eb5297e/full)).

Three design rules follow, though they are inferences, not formal bounds: no paper found gives a dimensions-per-comparisons rule. First, **about 4 continuous or ordinal dimensions is the defensible ceiling for pure pairwise preferential BO** at a crowd-worker budget of roughly 15–30 comparisons. Second, the screening data should be reused as a population prior or warm start, which the Meta-PO and group-level results support even though Jansen et al. (2025) found no final benefit. Third, the four should be selected by explicit criteria: (a) importance, meaning the BWS mean utility; (b) **heterogeneity**, meaning variance of individual-level BWS scores or latent-class splits, since only high-variance properties gain from personalisation; (c) monotone or single-peaked behaviour along a continuous axis; and (d) independence from the other three. Categorical properties such as highlight style and colour scheme should be settled during screening and then fixed, because pairwise BO handles them poorly and they cannot be interpolated between clips.

## Fifteen video-editable properties enter screening

The screening list applies five criteria. Every property is **editable in a pre-recorded first-person clip as a post-production overlay** (a hard gate). Each has a perceivable low-vs-high contrast within a short clip. Each is not already settled by consensus. Each has evidence of disagreement, or is an untested gap with a plausible preference split. And together they cover the four display families: path, road users, explanation and vehicle state. Several properties are deliberately novel relative to Jansen et al. (2025) (look-ahead length, path style, manoeuvre cue, colour scheme, explanation text detail, persistence policy, confidence indicator). Results on them will extend the literature rather than replicate it.

| # | Property (catalogue ID) | Low clip version | High clip version | Reason for inclusion | Fit as one of ~4 BO dimensions |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ego path opacity, off as the floor (P1+P2) | Path absent, or faint line at alpha ≈0.1–0.2 | Solid line at alpha ≈0.9–1 | Trust effect flips sign across studies; expert SD 0.26; users split on wanting it | **Strong**: continuous |
| 2 | Ego path width (P3) | Thin centre line | Vehicle-width translucent carpet | A tested range exists (Jansen et al. 2025); clutter concern means a real trade-off | Good: continuous |

Three practical notes make these clips work. Persistence (#14) needs clips that contain a distinct event, such as a crossing pedestrian or a lane change, so that "event-only" and "always-on" actually differ on screen. Clip length should include the 3–5 s lead before the event, consistent with fixing "explanation before action" ([Du 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.08878)). The intention marker (#11) and confidence dip (#15) must match what the recorded road users and situation actually do, because inaccurate content is penalised more than missing content ([Kaufman 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05731v3)). And all non-manipulated properties should sit at a neutral default taken from the Jansen et al. 2025 expert means (path alpha 0.71, path size 0.34, highlight alpha 0.23, marker size 0.15) ([Jansen 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06757v1)), so that A/B pairs differ in exactly one property.

The reserve list holds four further editable properties that can substitute for weak performers in a pilot: background dimming (G6, an untested gap), distance/TTC labels (R9), vehicle-status panel opacity (V3), and screen-fixed panel position (G3). They miss the main list because their evidence is thinner, or because their effect overlaps with an included property: dimming overlaps opacity and contrast, and V3 overlaps the confidence panel.

## Conclusion

The literature points to a two-tier display grammar. **Meaning should be standardised** (what colour signals, when explanations appear, where graphics live, line vs cone). **Volume should be personalised** (how opaque, how many objects, how much text, how persistent). This also predicts which properties will come out of the screening as best for personalisation: highlight opacity, highlight scope, explanation detail and persistence policy, with ego-path opacity as the strongest challenger. Each is continuous or ordinal, each has documented between-person disagreement, and together they span perception, explanation and timing rather than four knobs on one element. If the BWS heterogeneity estimates confirm this, the optimisation stage will test the hypothesis that Jansen et al. (2025) could not isolate in 16 dimensions: that the gains come from tuning the *amount* of transparency to the person.

The biggest open risk is the objective, not the dimensionality. Pairwise preference rewards what feels reassuring, and three independent lines of evidence (overtrust in Colley et al. 2022, error sensitivity in Kaufman et al. 2025, calibration-not-boost in the uncertainty work) warn that reassurance and understanding can diverge. A study that pairs its preference optimisation with a short comprehension or predictability probe per clip would be the first to show whether personalised transparency displays stay honest, not just liked.
